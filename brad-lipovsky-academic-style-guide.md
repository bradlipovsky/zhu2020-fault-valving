# Brad Lipovsky Academic Writing Style Guide

Use this guide when drafting, revising, or reviewing academic writing in Brad Lipovsky's voice. It is based on the supplied publication corpus:

- Lipovsky and Dunham, 2015, "Vibrational modes of hydraulic fractures"
- Lipovsky and Dunham, 2016, "Tremor during ice-stream stick slip"
- Lipovsky and Dunham, 2017, "Slow-slip events on the Whillans Ice Plain"
- Lipovsky, 2018, "Ice Shelf Rift Propagation and the Mechanics of Wave-Induced Fracture"
- Lipovsky et al., 2019, "Glacier sliding, seismicity and sediment entrainment"
- Lipovsky, 2020, "Ice shelf rift propagation: stability, three-dimensional effects, and the role of marginal weakening"
- Graff et al., 2025, "Calving-driven fjord dynamics resolved by seafloor fibre sensing"

Weight the sole- and lead-author papers most heavily. Use the 2025 Nature paper as evidence for how the same scientific instincts appear in a recent, highly compressed, collaborative style.

## Core Voice

Brad's academic style is mechanics-first, restrained, and explanatory. The prose turns observations into physical constraints, then uses those constraints to evaluate a model, hypothesis, or mechanism. The tone is confident when describing calculations and data, but careful when moving from model behavior to Earth-system interpretation.

Prioritize:

1. A clear physical mechanism.
2. A visible bridge between observation and model.
3. Precise claim strength.
4. Quantitative scale.
5. Understated implications.

Do not write like a proposal abstract, press release, or broad review. Avoid generic urgency. The writing should feel like a careful scientist showing exactly how a hidden mechanical process can be inferred from observable quantities.

## Argument Pattern

Most papers follow this logic:

1. Start from an important Earth-system process.
2. Identify a specific uncertainty or disagreement.
3. Explain why direct observation is difficult or incomplete.
4. Introduce an observable signal, laboratory result, or simplified model that can constrain the hidden process.
5. State the physical assumptions before the mathematical details.
6. Compare model behavior with observations.
7. Interpret the mismatch, threshold, or scaling relation.
8. End with a bounded implication and a testable next step.

Use causal connectives deliberately. "Therefore" should mark a real consequence, not a transition. "However" should introduce a mechanical or observational tension. "Although" is often the cleanest way to acknowledge a limitation without losing the thread.

## Section-Level Style

### Titles

Titles usually name the process and the physical lens. Prefer direct noun phrases over clever or question-style titles.

Good patterns:

- "[Process] and the mechanics of [mechanism]"
- "[Observation] described using [physical law/model]"
- "[Process]: [stability/effects/role of mechanism]"
- "[Observable], [process], and [inferred condition]"

Avoid titles that promise a broad climate conclusion unless the paper actually closes that loop.

### Abstracts

Use the abstract as a compact causal chain. A typical structure is:

1. Broad process and why it matters.
2. Specific unresolved mechanism or disagreement.
3. "Here, I/we..." statement of method.
4. Physical model, assumption, or data source.
5. Main result, with scale or threshold when available.
6. Interpretation of what the result suggests.
7. Bounded implication for observations, modeling, or future work.

Brad's abstracts often move quickly from context to mechanics. Do not spend many sentences on literature history. Include the relevant physical object early: rift, ice shelf, ice stream, glacier bed, hydraulic fracture, fjord, waveguide, fault patch.

Use active contribution verbs:

- "I quantify..."
- "I infer..."
- "I relate..."
- "I present..."
- "We use..."
- "We show..."
- "We test..."
- "We propose the hypothesis..."

### Introductions

Open with a concrete geophysical consequence, then narrow rapidly.

Preferred opening move:

1. "[System/process] affects [larger consequence]."
2. "[Specific process] controls or limits that system."
3. "However/Despite..., [mechanical origin or controlling condition] remains unclear."
4. "[Observation or model] provides a way to constrain it."

Introductions should not merely say that a topic is important. They should establish why a particular mechanical question is the bottleneck.

Use citations densely in the context-setting paragraphs. Use them to anchor observational facts, previous models, and known disagreements. When the paper's own contribution begins, reduce citation density and make the new logic easy to follow.

Close the introduction by stating the contribution and, when useful, previewing the paper's structure:

- "Here, I/we [model/measure/test]..."
- "This approach [connects observable A to physical quantity B]."
- "The paper is organized as follows..." is acceptable when the model/data/results sequence is complex.

### Plain Language Summaries

When writing for a journal that asks for a plain-language summary, use direct analogies sparingly and keep the physical claim intact. The summary should translate scale, setting, and mechanism, not dilute the result.

Good plain-language elements:

- State the observable phenomenon in everyday terms.
- Give the spatial and temporal scale.
- Explain the model analogy only as far as needed.
- End with why the mechanism matters for larger simulations or forecasts.

### Methods and Model Sections

State assumptions before equations. The reader should know what idealized object is being modeled before seeing the governing relations.

A good model paragraph usually does four things:

1. Defines the geometry and coordinate system.
2. Names the governing law or constitutive relation.
3. States the approximation or limit.
4. Explains what observable quantity the model will predict or invert.

Use equations as load-bearing pieces of the argument. Define symbols close to first use. After an equation, interpret it in words: what controls the sign, scale, stability, attenuation, or threshold?

Common model moves:

- Compare limiting cases.
- Use nondimensional ratios to separate regimes.
- Derive or cite a stability criterion.
- Convert a measured signal into stress, friction, pressure, aperture, length, or effective pressure.
- Put long derivations, stability analysis, or numerical details in appendices.

Avoid presenting equations as an uninterrupted block. The prose should keep explaining what each mathematical step buys.

### Results

Results sections are figure-driven. Each paragraph should usually begin by saying what is being compared, what the main trend is, or what the calculation tests.

Use this pattern:

1. "Figure N shows [quantity] as a function of [control parameter]."
2. "The main feature is [trend, threshold, sign change, or mismatch]."
3. "This behavior occurs because [mechanism]."
4. "It implies [bounded inference]."

Prefer specific physical language over generic performance language. Say "the model reproduces the timing but not the propagation direction" rather than "the model performs well."

### Discussion

The discussion should actively manage uncertainty. Brad's papers do not hide simplifying assumptions; they name them, estimate their effect, and separate robust conclusions from speculative ones.

Useful discussion subsections:

- Uncertainties in a calculation.
- Competing hypotheses.
- Physical interpretation of a parameter.
- Observational tests.
- Implications for larger-scale models.
- Limits of transfer from simplified geometry to real geometry.

When discussing alternatives, do not caricature them. Explain what each alternative would predict and how future data could distinguish it.

### Conclusions

Conclusions are concise and cumulative. They should not introduce a broad new claim. The usual sequence is:

1. "I/we have [done the calculation/model/analysis]."
2. "The central result is [specific result]."
3. "This result suggests [interpretation], subject to [main limitation]."
4. "Future [observation/modeling] could test [specific uncertainty]."
5. End with a modest broader significance.

Good final implications sound like:

- "This is a step toward..."
- "These observations provide a way to..."
- "This relationship may help constrain..."
- "Future deployments could clarify..."

Avoid ending with an oversized claim about solving sea-level rise, glacier forecasting, or climate prediction.

## Claim Strength

Keep the distinction between observation, calculation, inference, and speculation explicit.

Use:

- "observed", "measured", "recorded" for data.
- "calculate", "estimate", "model", "simulate" for analysis.
- "predicts", "reproduces", "is consistent with" for model-data comparison.
- "suggests", "implies", "may indicate", "is probably due to" for inference.
- "could", "may", "would", "potentially" for speculative mechanisms or future applications.

Do not write "demonstrates" unless the paper truly rules out the important alternatives. Do not write "proves." A model result should not be phrased as a direct observation of nature.

Good:

- "The observations are consistent with local changes in effective pressure."
- "The model predicts instability when the rift exits the embayment."
- "This mismatch suggests that an additional strengthening mechanism is present."

Avoid:

- "The observations prove that effective pressure controls the system."
- "The model reveals the true mechanism."
- "This discovery transforms our understanding..."

## Sentence Style

The typical sentence is moderately long, often around 18-24 words, with shorter sentences used for topic statements and conclusions. Long technical sentences are acceptable when they preserve a clear chain of cause and effect.

Use a mix of:

- Direct topic sentences.
- Conditional sentences with "if", "when", and "although."
- Mechanistic explanations joined by "therefore", "because", and "in contrast."
- Short interpretive sentences after equations or figures.

Common transition words that fit this style:

- "however"
- "although"
- "therefore"
- "in contrast"
- "in particular"
- "specifically"
- "for example"
- "nevertheless"

Use these only when the logical relation is real.

Avoid:

- Marketing adjectives: "groundbreaking", "transformative", "unprecedented" unless literally justified.
- Vague intensifiers: "very", "highly", "extremely" when a number or scale can be given.
- Generic openers: "Recently, there has been growing interest..."
- Rhetorical questions.

## Paragraph Style

A good paragraph has one mechanical job. It should not be a list of related facts.

Preferred structure:

1. Topic sentence naming the process or question.
2. Evidence, model setup, or previous result.
3. Causal interpretation.
4. Consequence for the next paragraph.

Brad often uses paragraphs to move from a broad process to a specific control parameter. Maintain that direction. If a paragraph contains both context and a result, split it.

## Figures and Captions

Captions should be self-contained enough that a reader can understand the figure's purpose without searching the main text. They often do more than label panels; they state the comparison and the physical interpretation.

A good caption includes:

- What each panel shows.
- Data source or model setup.
- Units and normalization.
- Meaning of colors, symbols, and line styles.
- Key parameter values when needed.
- The main physical takeaway if the figure is central.

In the main text, refer to figures as evidence:

- "Figure N shows..."
- "This comparison indicates..."
- "The dashed curve gives the limiting behavior..."
- "The departure from the analytical limit occurs when..."

Avoid decorative figures. Every figure should either establish an observation, validate a model, compare regimes, or support a mechanistic inference.

## Quantitative and Technical Conventions

Use quantitative scale early and often. Brad's writing commonly anchors claims with sizes, durations, velocities, pressures, frequencies, lengths, or recurrence times.

Use:

- SI units unless the target journal requires otherwise.
- Approximate values where exact values are not warranted.
- Parenthetical scale reminders: "10-100 km", "about 30 min", "order of magnitude", "twice daily."
- Defined acronyms on first use.
- Symbols only after the physical quantity is named.
- "necessary but not sufficient" when discussing conditions that enable but do not guarantee behavior.

For equations:

- Define variables close to first use.
- Explain the limiting case in prose.
- State whether a criterion is empirical, analytical, numerical, or assumed.
- Give the physical meaning of nondimensional groups.
- When delimiters are nested, order them from outermost to innermost as `\{ [ ( ) ] \}`. Use square brackets for column vectors and matrices, and parentheses for function arguments.
- Use appendices for derivations that would interrupt the main argument.

## Citations

Citations should support specific claims. They are not decoration.

Use author-year names in prose when the study itself is part of the argument:

- "[Author] showed that..."
- "[Author] observed..."
- "[Author] proposed..."

Use parenthetical citations for background facts or clustered evidence. Keep citation clusters relevant and not too long.

The introduction usually carries the highest citation density. Results and discussion should cite when comparing to previous observations, models, or mechanisms, but the paper's own logic should be foregrounded.

## Reusable Templates

### Abstract Contribution

"Here, I/we [quantify/test/model] [mechanism] by [method or data]. This approach relates [observable] to [physical quantity], allowing [hypothesis/threshold] to be evaluated."

### Introduction Gap

"Although [process] is known to [effect], the mechanical conditions that control [specific behavior] remain uncertain. This uncertainty persists because [direct observation/model limitation]."

### Model Setup

"I/we model [system] as [idealized geometry/material] subject to [loading/boundary condition]. This idealization isolates [mechanism] while retaining [essential physical effect]."

### Result Paragraph

"Figure N shows [quantity] as a function of [control]. The main feature is [trend/threshold]. This behavior occurs because [mechanism], and therefore [bounded consequence]."

### Discussion Limitation

"This calculation makes several simplifying assumptions. The most important is [assumption], which affects [quantity] through [mechanism]. Nevertheless, [robust result] remains because [reason]."

### Conclusion

"I/we have [method] to evaluate [mechanism]. The central result is [specific finding]. This result suggests [interpretation], but [limitation/alternative] remains possible. Future [observations/models] could distinguish between [alternatives]."

## Brad-Like Revision Checklist

Before considering a draft finished, check:

1. Does the opening identify a specific mechanical bottleneck rather than only a broad motivation?
2. Is the main observable connected to a physical quantity?
3. Are assumptions stated before equations?
4. Does every equation receive a physical interpretation?
5. Are model predictions separated from observations?
6. Are uncertainty and competing mechanisms named explicitly?
7. Are claims hedged only where needed, and not hedged where the calculation is direct?
8. Does each figure support a claim in the text?
9. Do captions explain panels, units, symbols, and the main comparison?
10. Are scales, units, and thresholds included where they matter?
11. Does the conclusion restate the central result without overselling it?
12. Is the final implication a bounded next step rather than a sweeping claim?

## Things to Avoid

Avoid:

- "This paper explores..." when the paper actually quantifies, tests, or constrains.
- Broad novelty language without a specific physical contribution.
- Conclusions that sound more certain than the data-model comparison allows.
- Long literature summaries that do not sharpen the mechanical question.
- Purely qualitative claims when a scale, threshold, or sign can be stated.
- Treating a simplified model as a complete Earth-system model.
- Hiding uncertainty in passive voice.

Prefer:

- "This model predicts..."
- "The observations suggest..."
- "The calculation implies..."
- "This result is consistent with..."
- "Future observations could test..."
